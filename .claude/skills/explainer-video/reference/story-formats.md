# Story formats

A vocabulary of shapes for short explainers, not a menu to pick from blindly. The right shape
comes out of the topic's own structure: is there a misconception, a route, a number nobody can
picture, a puzzle, a pair people confuse, a history that explains a present oddity? Combine
formats freely (a myth-buster hook on a zoom-journey body with a scale-reveal payoff), or invent
one and describe it in the brief.

Proportions below are for a 60–90 s video; stretch the middle for longer ones.

## Choosing

Ask these of the research before choosing:

| If the topic has… | consider |
|---|---|
| a belief most viewers hold that is wrong | Myth → truth; Debunk the diagram |
| nested scales (whole → part → smaller part) | Zoom journey |
| something that travels through a system | Follow the thing; You are… |
| a design that follows from constraints | Build it from nothing |
| a counterfactual that exposes why it matters | What if |
| a number too big or small to feel | Scale reveal |
| two things people confuse or compare | Versus |
| an anomaly with a satisfying cause | Mystery |
| a strong everyday parallel | The analogy world |
| an odd present explained by its past | Origin story |
| a sequence of steps | Recipe |
| several popular sub-questions | Three questions |
| many comparable cases | Countdown |

Then ask what the audience should *feel* — wonder, vindication, alarm, delight, "oh, that's
clever" — and prefer the shape that delivers that feeling in its last ten seconds.

## Length

Pick the shortest length that delivers the angle. One idea and one twist: 30–45 s. A mechanism:
60–90 s. A system with several interacting parts, for an audience that chose to learn: up to
150 s. Long videos need more structural surprises, not more content.

## Myth → truth

**Signals.** A widely held belief about the topic is wrong or half right.
**Hook.** State the myth as the viewer believes it, in picture and words, and undercut it within
three seconds ("…and that's not how it works").
**Shape.** Myth (5–8 %) → why it seems true (10–15 %) → the real mechanism, shown (40 %) → proof or
demonstration (20 %) → what changes now that you know (10 %) → callback to the myth's image (5 %).
**Visuals.** Draw the myth the way people imagine it, then transform that same picture into the
true one — the transformation is the explanation.
**Risks.** Smugness; never mock the viewer for believing it. The truth must be as drawable as the myth.
**Example.** "Thousands of cores" → lanes in lockstep.

## Zoom journey

**Signals.** Nested scales: city → building → pipe; body → cell → protein; chip → unit → lane.
**Hook.** Start at the familiar scale and ask a question only the smallest scale answers.
**Shape.** One beat per level (3–5 levels), each answering "what is this made of / how does it
work", then zoom all the way back out and see the whole with new eyes.
**Visuals.** Continuous camera; `zoomIn` transitions aimed at the part that becomes the next scene;
the hero object reappears at each level.
**Risks.** "And inside that…" monotony: give every level its own reveal and its own surprise.

## Follow the thing

**Signals.** Something travels: a packet, a letter, an electron, a bite of food, a dollar.
**Hook.** Introduce the traveller with a destination and a clock ("This message has 0.2 seconds
to cross the planet").
**Shape.** Departure → 3–6 stations in order (only the surprising ones) → arrival → zoom out to scale
("now do that a billion times a second").
**Visuals.** A recurring token or character, a route that draws itself, timestamps, a camera that
follows.
**Risks.** Too many stations. Cut every station that does not change the traveller.

## You are… (second person)

**Signals.** The subject is a small actor inside a big system (a red blood cell, a photon, a
raindrop).
**Hook.** "You are a photon. You've just left the Sun. You have eight minutes."
**Shape.** Stakes and clock → obstacles experienced from inside → the moment the mechanism saves or
transforms you → the reveal of where you ended up.
**Visuals.** The camera is the character; a status HUD of the character's own (speed, energy,
time left) instead of chapter names. Other characters and obstacles can be Lottie animations,
reacting as you pass.
**Risks.** Fake stakes feel gimmicky; the stakes must be the real physics or biology.

## Build it from nothing

**Signals.** An engineered thing whose design follows from its constraints.
**Hook.** "Let's invent X from scratch. Rule one: …" or a problem the viewer would also fail at.
**Shape.** Problem → naive solution → why it fails, shown → fix → the fix's new problem → fix …
(3–4 rounds) → the real design appears, assembled from the fixes.
**Visuals.** The object assembles piece by piece; each fix adds a part in its own colour and stays
on screen, so the final design is a visible history of the reasoning.
**Risks.** Every failure must be shown, not told; skipping a step breaks the logic.

## What if

**Signals.** A counterfactual exposes why the real thing matters.
**Hook.** The question, with the picture already changing ("What if the Moon vanished tonight?").
**Shape.** Setup → first consequence → escalation in 2–4 steps, each bigger → the limit or twist →
what it tells us about reality.
**Visuals.** A simulation feel: before/after states, a running clock, a counter.
**Risks.** Speculation passing as fact. Label estimates as estimates; source the physics.

## Scale reveal

**Signals.** The core fact is a number nobody can feel.
**Hook.** The number with one comparison that lands.
**Shape.** Number → 2–3 escalating comparisons → how it is even possible (the mechanism) → its
cost or limit.
**Visuals.** Big type, stacking, multiplying objects, counters that land on the spoken number.
**Risks.** Every comparison must be computed and checked; write the arithmetic in sources.md.

## Versus

**Signals.** Two things people confuse or compare (virus vs bacterium, LED vs OLED, CPU vs GPU).
**Hook.** Both side by side and a question whose answer surprises.
**Shape.** Introduce the contestants → 2–3 rounds, one dimension each → verdict ("it depends on…")
→ the deeper reason they differ.
**Visuals.** One colour per side, kept through the whole video; a scoreboard. In a vertical frame,
stack them, and alternate for details rather than shrinking both.
**Risks.** Split screens halve what can be read; never run two detailed diagrams at once.

## Mystery

**Signals.** An anomaly with a satisfying cause (why sunsets are red; why ice floats).
**Hook.** Present the anomaly as evidence: "This shouldn't happen."
**Shape.** The anomaly → clue 1 → clue 2 → the obvious suspect, eliminated → the real cause → the
reveal ties every clue together. Reveal by 70–80 % of the runtime.
**Visuals.** Evidence cards, a case board, spotlight lighting (`stage` backdrop), a magnifier.
**Risks.** Suspense without payoff; each clue must be a real piece of the answer.

## The analogy world

**Signals.** An abstract system with a strong everyday parallel (immune system ↔ security team;
GPU ↔ a kitchen brigade).
**Hook.** Open inside the analogy without naming the topic, then reveal ("This kitchen is inside
your graphics card").
**Shape.** Set up the world → map 3–4 roles → run the process inside the world → show where the
analogy breaks and what the real thing does instead → return to reality.
**Visuals.** An illustrated world with recurring characters and props — Lottie characters from one
author keep it consistent; real-world names appear as labels and captions.
**Risks.** The analogy becomes the topic. Pay back the real mechanism, and show where the analogy
fails — that is what keeps it honest.

## Origin story

**Signals.** The present only makes sense through its history (why QWERTY, why 60 Hz).
**Hook.** The odd present-day fact, and "it's because of something that happened in 1874".
**Shape.** Present oddity → origin moment → 2–4 turning points as a chain of causes → back to the
present → what comes next.
**Visuals.** A timeline, dates as big type, the look shifting subtly with each era.
**Risks.** A list of dates. Every step must be "and so…", not "and then…".

## Recipe

**Signals.** A clear sequence of steps (how a vaccine is made, how a plane lands).
**Hook.** The end state and its cost or time ("From needle to immunity in fourteen days").
**Shape.** 4–7 numbered steps, one visual action each → a fast recap montage.
**Visuals.** Numbered chapters (the segmented progress bar earns its place here), a process line
that fills as steps complete.
**Risks.** Every step with the same structure is monotonous: vary the visual move per step.

## Three questions

**Signals.** Several popular sub-questions about one topic.
**Hook.** The most-asked question on screen.
**Shape.** Q1 → A1 (fast) → Q2 → A2 → Q3, the hardest → A3 → the thread connecting all three.
**Visuals.** Question cards that flip; one consistent Q/A rhythm.
**Risks.** A scattered video. Choose questions that build on each other.

## Countdown

**Signals.** Many comparable cases ranked on one clear dimension.
**Hook.** Show that #1 is surprising ("#1 isn't what you think").
**Shape.** 3–5 items, least to most surprising, 8–15 s each with one fact and one picture; #1 gets
double time.
**Visuals.** Numbered cards from one template, a tally.
**Risks.** List fatigue. Five items at most, and every one needs a picture-worthy twist.

## Debunk the diagram

**Signals.** A famous simplified diagram that misleads (the Bohr atom, the tongue map).
**Hook.** The familiar diagram — redrawn in the video's own style, never copied — then it cracks.
**Shape.** The familiar picture → what it gets right → where it breaks → the better picture → why the
simple one survives.
**Visuals.** The old diagram transforms into the new one.
**Risks.** "It's complicated" is not a replacement: the better picture must be clear.

## Pacing presets

Decide one in the brief, then write its pauses into `script.py` (the number after each take) and
its voice speed into `SPEED`. Figures measured by re-recording the GPU video's 390-word script:

| Preset | `SPEED` | Pause inside a beat | At a beat change | Overall pace (measured / modelled) | Suits |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| calm | 1.0 | 0.35–0.55 s | 0.7–0.8 s | ≈ 147–154 wpm | wonder, gravity, dense mechanisms |
| brisk | 1.05–1.1 | 0.2–0.35 s | 0.45–0.6 s | ≈ 159–168 wpm | most explainers — the better default |
| rapid | 1.1–1.15 | 0.12–0.25 s | 0.3–0.45 s | ≈ 167–178 wpm | lists, myth-busters, comedy |

What the measurements say:

- **Voice speed is a weak lever.** `SPEED` 1.1 made the speech 5 % shorter, 1.2 made it 11 %
  shorter (170 → 180 → 193 words per minute while speaking), because pauses inside a take do not
  compress. 1.1 transcribed exactly as cleanly as 1.0; at 1.2 the voice starts to blur consonants
  (word error 0.25 % → 0.76 %: "scratchpad" heard as "scratch bed"). Stay at or below 1.15.
- **Fewer words is the strong lever.** A video feels fast when every sentence earns its place and
  the picture changes often — not when the voice hurries. Cutting 390 words to 230 takes a 160 s
  video to about 90 s at the same, comfortable delivery.
- Inline `{p:…}` pauses and commas count as speaking time: in a rapid video, use them only where the
  pause *is* the joke or the reveal.

The reference GPU video is *calm* (147 wpm overall over 159 s of speech) and was judged too long
and too slow: *brisk* at 60–100 s is the better default.

## Every format needs

- A hook that is a picture and a line, both inside three seconds.
- An open loop the hook creates and the ending closes.
- A structural surprise every 15–25 seconds: a change of scale, of format, of colour register, a
  full-screen word, a hard cut — something that resets attention.
- An ending that connects to the start (callback, loop, or answered question), and a final image
  that holds while the music resolves.
