# Creative brief — “How Painkillers Work”

## Audience and promise

Adults who take ibuprofen or paracetamol now and then, for headaches, sprains or periods, and who
half-believe the tablet travels to the sore spot. They have heard that it "blocks inflammation" and
picture a targeted fix. After about 85 seconds they can say why a painkiller cannot aim, why the
same drug reaches the stomach and the kidneys, why the sore spot still looks like the target, and
that not every painkiller works through the same chemistry. They stop scrolling because the opening
claim, "your painkiller knows exactly where it hurts", is one they have probably believed themselves.
The promise is an explanation that changes what they think happens when they take one.

## Angle

**How the topic is usually told.** Checked from write-ups and from video titles and summaries; no
video was downloaded or watched. The Howitworksdaily article (2013) already makes our premise: "So,
painkillers don't 'know' – they go everywhere, reaching the damaged cells in the process." Video
explainers (George Zaidan's TED-Ed lesson "How Do Pain Relievers Work?", Sabine Hossenfelder's
painkiller videos, a biochemistry.org resource) explain prostaglandins and the COX enzymes, mostly as
mechanism or as a family-by-family comparison. The default picture, inferred from those summaries, is
a tablet shown travelling to the injury and blocking an enzyme there. So the premise alone is not the
angle. The angle must explain why the drug still looks targeted, and what the same mechanism costs.

**Five candidate angles, one sentence each:**
1. A painkiller doesn't find your pain: it floods the bloodstream, and the sore spot only looks like the target because the chemical it blocks is made fastest there.
2. The tablet that quiets your sprained ankle also weakens your stomach's protection and your kidneys' blood supply.
3. Your injury doesn't call the painkiller; it just happens to be where the enzyme is busiest.
4. There is no single painkiller trick: an anti-inflammatory works through the body's chemistry, paracetamol mostly through the brain and spinal cord, and opioids through the nervous system.
5. The homing-tablet picture is the most common and the most wrong thing people believe about painkillers.

**Kept: 1.** It is true (the label's peak time, and COX-2 switching on in hurt tissue), surprising
(the targeting picture is the one people hold), and drawable (a flood map and a busy factory). Its
payoff is 2, the costs, which gives the viewer a reason to care. 4 becomes the "where it breaks"
beat. Lost: 2 on its own is a warning, not an explanation. 3 is the core of 1 and is folded into it.
5 is a frame rather than a mechanism, so it shapes the form of the video rather than its angle.

## Directions considered

### A — Myth to truth, as an anatomy plate (chosen, with one device from B)

The first frame is a tablet with a targeting crosshair locking onto a sprained ankle, drawn in the
plate's ink lines, with the line "Your painkiller knows where it hurts." Within three seconds
the crosshair shatters into blue dots that spread through a line-drawn loop of vessels across the
whole body. The rest is a careful walk through one body map: the flood, the busy factory, the costs,
and the places where the myth breaks. It suits the topic because the picture viewers already hold
(targeting) is transformed into the true one, and the transformation is the explanation. Biggest
risk: a diagram-first video can read as a textbook page unless every beat contains a visual
transformation rather than a list.

### B — Follow the molecule, as a pastel transit map

A single illustrated tablet with a face sets off from the mouth, rides a pastel transit map through
the body, and stops at four stations (ankle, stomach, kidneys, clotting cells), each a small story.
The tone is playful and warm; the look is candy-pop. It suits the "everywhere" message, because the
stations make the travel visible. Biggest risk: the cheerful tone undersells the stomach and kidney
warnings, and a talking pill reads as a gimmick.

### C — Mystery on a dark stage

A case file: "A tablet that goes everywhere relieves one sprained ankle. Why?" Evidence cards: the drug
is in the blood; relief shows only at the ankle; the clue; the reveal (the busy factory and the cost).
Dark stage, one warm light on the ankle, serif headlines, slow fades. It suits a forensic tone and
holds suspense. Biggest risk: the answer arrives late, the dark body map is murky on a phone, and the
myth is never named outright.

The three differ in story shape (myth, follow-the-thing, mystery), in visual world (anatomy diagram,
transit map, case board), in tone (wry, playful, forensic) and in look (paper, candy, dark stage).

## Chosen direction

A, with one device from B. What came from where: the myth-to-truth frame, the body-map diagram and the
transformation logic come from A. The single tablet that travels the bloodstream comes from B, without
the transit map or the faces. The question "how does it find the ankle?" from C is used only as the
open loop of the hook, and is closed at the end. A beats B because the costs need a serious voice that
a playful look would undercut. A beats C because the myth is named and undercut inside three seconds,
and the explanation arrives in the middle of the video, not at the end.

## Hook

Three openings, each a picture and a line, both inside three seconds:
1. A tablet's crosshair locks onto a sprained ankle drawn in ink lines. Line: "Your painkiller knows where it hurts." At about 3 s the crosshair shatters into blue dots that spread through the body. Line: "It doesn't."
2. A bruised ankle alone in the frame, the rest of the body faded. Line: "Why does a tablet that goes everywhere fix just one ankle?"
3. A stomach outline with a shield icon that cracks. Line: "The tablet that fixed your ankle may be weakening your stomach."

**Chosen: 1.** It states the viewer's belief and undercuts it within the three-second limit, and the
transformation is itself the first explanation. Its open loop is "then how does it work there?", which
is closed by the busy-factory reveal and by the callback at the end. Option 2 survives as the open loop
of the middle section. Option 3 becomes the opening line of the cost beat.

## Story shape

**Format:** myth to truth (story-formats.md), with the follow-the-thing route inside the flood beat and
a "where the myth breaks" beat late in the video.

**Beat map (93 s as recorded; times are the measured scene starts):**
1. **THE MYTH (0–3.8 s).** The crosshair locks onto the ankle. "Your painkiller knows where it hurts." Then "It doesn't." The crosshair shatters. Turn: the viewer must see the body differently.
2. **WHY IT SEEMS TRUE (3.8–9.9 s).** The ankle improves and the crosshair seems right. "It seems to." Turn: the real route is needed.
3. **THE FLOOD (9.9–24.0 s).** A zoom out to the whole body. The tablet dissolves, the drug enters the blood, and the loop fills with blue dots. The level peaks within one to two hours, and the drug sticks to blood protein. Pattern interrupt: the scale change at 10 s.
4. **THE FACTORY (24.0–43.4 s).** A zoom into the ankle. Hurt tissue switches on the enzyme COX-2, and the factories light up in vermilion and throw off prostaglandin marks, which make the nerves more sensitive. Blue block bars appear across the factories in every organ at once. The ankle's factories lose the most. Pattern interrupt: the zoom in at 24 s, the colour change at 43 s.
5. **THE COST (43.4–62.5 s).** The same block in the stomach (its protective shield thins) and the kidneys (flow arrows slow), in alert red. The label calls the stomach risks serious. Pattern interrupt: red appears only in this beat, and the transition fades to ink.
6. **WHERE THE MYTH BREAKS (62.5–80.4 s).** A fork in the route: paracetamol and opioid arrows go to a line-drawn brain and spinal cord instead of to the factories. Pattern interrupt: the fork at 62 s. The uncertainty ("still argued over") is stated in the voice.
7. **THE TRUTH (80.4–93.1 s).** The crosshair returns, empty. "So it doesn't know where your pain is. It doesn't need to." The factories dim; the blue dots stay in the blood. The end card holds: "Read the label." and "Ask a pharmacist if you have stomach ulcers or kidney problems."

The ending mirrors the start: the crosshair comes back empty, and the first line is answered. The
open loop from the hook ("then how does it work there?") is closed by the busy-factory reveal in beat 4.

## Pacing

Brisk. Voice af_heart at SPEED 1.07. About 224 words: roughly 90 s of speech and pauses, plus a 0.3 s
lead-in and a 2.6 s end card, for 93 s in total (measured by `lockstep voice`). Pauses are 0.2–0.35 s inside a beat, 0.45–0.6 s
between thoughts, and 0.7–0.8 s at a beat change, which is where the scene changes. The picture changes
at least every 4–5 seconds. On-screen figures ("one to two hours") stay up for at least 2.5 seconds, so
they can be read on a phone. Fast is not rushed: the factory beat is the longest, because the viewer
needs time to read the labels on the body map, and the costs and the callback get the longer pauses.

## Visual world

The world is an anatomical field-guide page: one line-drawn body map in ink, and the drug is the only
blue element that moves. The hero object is the tablet, the library's pill illustration tinted in the
subject blue, with a springy hop on arrival. Diagram, object and type are kept distinct: diagram for
the body, the blood loop and the factories; object for the tablet and the ankle (the library's foot
illustration); type for the numbers and the mono labels. The organs are custom line paths, because the
illustration set has no stomach or kidney (checked: "stomach" and "platelet" return no matches, and
"kidney" returns only beans). The brain comes from the library. Scale and time are shown by camera:
zoom-outs for the flood, zoom-ins for the factory. The drug's level is shown by how many blue dots
remain in the loop. The camera is steady and precise, with slow push-ins and no shake.

Deliberately left out: photographs, faces of real people, body horror, logos, brands and packaging.
The tablet is not named by brand.

## Style

**anatomy-plate** (style.json). Light, because body diagrams need the crisp dark ink of a printed page
to read on a phone, and because a medical explanation has to be trusted.

- **Palette and roles.** Subject ink-blue #1F5FBF: the drug, its route and the blue dots. Accent vermilion #D9480F: the factories, the prostaglandin marks and the numbers. Data violet #6B4EA0: organs and blood-family words. Alert deep red #B3261E: the cost only (the stomach shield, the kidney flow, the label's warnings). Good green #2B8A3E: the ankle recovering at the end. Ink #1F1B16 for text and the body outline. Ink contrast 13.4:1; every role at least 3.4:1 (style-check passes).
- **Fonts.** Fraunces for display (the headline and "It doesn't"), Lora for the body lines, Courier Prime for numbers and labels. One display face with character, one quiet reading face, one mono for figures.
- **Backdrop.** Warm paper, washed with the subject blue at low strength, with no ruled lines, because lines would compete with the diagram.
- **Captions.** Underline highlight, weight 800, stroke 12 for legibility on paper. Highlight classes follow the roles: k subject (the drug and mechanism words), n accent (numbers and quantities), m data (organs and blood), w alert (negation and warnings).
- **Transitions.** slideLeft at 0.7 s by default. A fade to ink at the cost beat, so the change of tone is visible. zoomIn for the move from the ankle to the body.
- **Shape.** Corner radius 10, outline 3 at 90 %, panels at 55 % opacity. No card shadows: a field guide has none.
- **Illustrations.** Line set, tint ink. Every illustration is one drawn line, like the body map.
  *(Note, 2026-11-16: the illustration set was removed from the engine after this video was made. The
  tablet, the warning sign and the brain are now the bundled line icons `pill`, `triangle-alert` and
  `brain` in the same colours and timing; the rest of this brief is unchanged.)*

**Rejected: chalk-sketch** (candidates/b.json, rendered and checked). It passes legibility, but its palette, fonts and backdrop are identical to the library's `chalkboard` (style-check warns). Its dark green board makes the grey line art murky at phone size, and its hand-lettered tone makes the cost beat feel like a classroom aside. **Not chosen: the dark stage** (midnight-editorial), the mystery direction's look. It was not rendered because direction C was not chosen.

**To check on the rendered frames:** the vermilion accent and the red alert must read as different roles on a phone, especially in the factory and cost beats. The tile shows them distinct.

**Previous video:** torn-qr-code used riso-sticker (cream stock, misregistered spot inks, die-cut stickers). This look shares a cream ground, but it has a serif display face, a line-art body, and no sticker borders or misregistration. The two should read as different.

## Sound

**Music:** pluck kit, 108 BPM, transpose −2 (a little darker and heavier for a medical topic). Moods follow the arc: THE MYTH drone (held, quiet); WHY IT SEEMS TRUE light; THE FLOOD groove (steady, with drive as the flood spreads); THE FACTORY mechanical (kick on every beat, room for the factory sounds); THE COST dark (half-time, minor); WHERE THE MYTH BREAKS tense (filtered, no kick, for limits); THE TRUTH finale (resolves to D major and holds after the last word). No two neighbouring chapters share a mood.

**Voice:** af_heart at SPEED 1.07. The research found that speeds above 1.15 blur consonants, and 1.07 keeps the body-map labels crisp.

**Effects:** a riser into the shatter at the hook, and an impact on "doesn't". Ticks as the blue dots spread; a thump as each factory lights. Whoosh for the zooms. A deny buzz on each block bar, and on "doesn't" in the hook and the callback. A low deny buzz as the stomach shield thins. A chime at the end card. Cues on short words sit 50–80 ms before the word at gain 0.5–0.6, so they never mask "it" or "not".

## What would make this fail

1. **The busy-factory idea reads as a measured result.** Prevention: the narration words it as a consequence ("the block removes the most where the enzyme is busiest"). The sources file calls it an inference, the report states it, and no per-tissue number appears on screen.
2. **The cost beat becomes a scare or medical advice.** Prevention: the costs are the label's own words, there are no incidence figures, and the only instruction is "Read the label" and "ask a pharmacist", which the label supports.
3. **The diagram becomes a textbook page.** Prevention: every beat has a visual transformation (the shatter, the flood, the factories lighting, the shield thinning, the fork, the empty crosshair), and no beat is a list.
4. **The dated 2005 label is taken as current.** Prevention: the only figure on screen is the peak window (one to two hours), which agrees with the label's pharmacokinetics. The report flags the label's date and recommends checking the current label before any re-publication.

## Retrospective

**What worked.** Deciding the direction before recording: the myth-to-truth shape gave the video its three-second undercut and its callback. The tile comparison picked the look with the eyes. The cue-driven scenes kept every picture on its word, and the voice check caught one misheard word before it reached the picture. Drawing the costs as changes to one body (the shield thins and cracks, the flow slows) worked better than any list would have.

**What did not.** Research took longer than the drawing, because several primary sources were behind paywalls or bot checks; next time, go straight to abstracts on Europe PMC and PMC. The 2005 label is the only ibuprofen label read. The factory dive crops the head, and the "busiest" marks are a schematic illustration rather than data. The first draft had 66 still stretches, and the motion fix came late; 14 short holds remain. The end-card subline is small on a phone. The mix was 21.7 dB before anyone checked it.

**Change next time.** Plan the idle-motion budget per scene at storyboard time, not after the first build. Check the speech-to-music ratio before the first render, and set the mix level then. Qualify every on-screen claim with the narration's own qualifier (the kidney label was a near miss). Check paper access early and fall back to abstracts at once.
