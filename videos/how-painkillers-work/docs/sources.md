Research comes before the script. Every number and every strong claim in the narration
or on screen must trace to an entry here. Prefer primary sources (vendor documentation,
standards, papers, official statistics) over summaries.

Accessed 2026-11-16. "Read" means the text itself was read (directly or through the abstract
record). "Bibliographic" means only the publication record was confirmed.

## 3. Deliberate simplifications

- **Prostaglandins** are one family, named once. The video does not name PGE2 or the receptor subtypes.
- **Ibuprofen is the example.** "Painkillers" are shown through three classes only: an NSAID, paracetamol and an opioid.
- **"Mostly travels in the blood"** is an inference from high plasma-protein binding and a small distribution volume. It was not measured per tissue.
- **"The busiest factory"**: the block removes the most where the enzyme is most active, because inhibition scales with enzyme activity. This is the mechanism's consequence, not a measured per-tissue result for ibuprofen. The narration says "most" only about where the enzyme is busiest. Labelled as an inference in the report.
- **"Everywhere"**: the block happens in every tissue the drug reaches that has COX. Tissue concentrations differ and are not quantified, so the video never claims equal strength everywhere.
- **Costs** are stated in the label's terms and the mechanism. No incidence numbers, because the only incidence figures I have are from a 2005 label and from prescription-dose populations.
- **Stomach**: the narration says prostaglandins "help protect the lining". Takeuchi places this beside hypermotility and microvascular mechanisms, and the video does not present one mechanism as the whole story.
- **No circulation time.** See section 4.
- **The 2005 label** is the only ibuprofen label read. Its numbers are used as the label's, not as current guidance.

## 4. Things checked and rejected

- **"Blood makes a full circuit of the body in about a minute."** Found only on secondary sites (a Pearson study-page reasoning from 5 L/min cardiac output and blood volume, plus Vedantu, Almanac and others giving about 45 s to a minute). OpenStax 19.4 gives 5.25 L/min per ventricle and no circuit time. **Rejected: not verified.** The script does not state a circulation time.
- **"Ibuprofen's inhibition is reversible; its platelet effect lasts only while the drug is in the blood."** Found on education pages and a TED-Ed summary. The NSAID review that would check it (PMC4508078) was not read. **Not used.** The platelet beat uses only the verified aspirin fact.
- **"Platelets live 7–10 days."** Secondary only. **Not used.**
- **"COX-2 is constitutively expressed in the kidney."** The review says the kidney data are unclear. **Not used.** The kidney beat uses the label's own renal finding.
- **"Paracetamol acts only in the brain."** The 2026 review argues it also acts peripherally. **Rejected as absolute;** the narration says "works mostly" in the brain and spinal cord and that "Scientists still argue about exactly how."
- **"Opioids block prostaglandins."** **Rejected:** they act through receptors. They are not in the prostaglandin pathway.
- **"Painkillers 'know' where pain is" as a targeting claim.** This is the premise the video refutes. The video never asserts targeting.
- **"Tuning chemicals"** (Howitworksdaily's phrase). **Not used.** The label's wording "sensitise tissues" is used instead.
- **Dates and doses from other labels or sites.** **Not used.** Only the 2005 monograph is the dosing source.

## 5. How this topic is usually told

Checked from titles, summaries and write-ups. I did not download or watch any video.

- **Write-ups.** The Howitworksdaily article (2013) makes the same premise: painkillers "don't know"; they go everywhere. It uses no analogy and does not explain why relief still looks local.
- **Video explainers.** George Zaidan's TED-Ed lesson "How Do Pain Relievers Work?" (family by family; a summary says it contrasts aspirin's irreversible effect with ibuprofen's). Sabine Hossenfelder's painkiller videos (NSAIDs block the enzymes behind prostaglandins). A biochemistry.org page (pain linked to prostaglandins that COX-2 helps cells produce after injury). The titles and summaries describe mechanism explainers and family comparisons.
- **The default picture** (inferred from those summaries, not from watching): a pill shown travelling to the injury and blocking an enzyme there, or a family-by-family comparison. This video avoids both. It draws the drug as a body-wide flood, shows the injury as where the enzyme is busiest, draws the costs, and shows where the framing breaks.
