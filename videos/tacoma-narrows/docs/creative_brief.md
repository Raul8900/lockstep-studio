# Creative brief — “The Bridge That Danced Itself to Death”

## Audience and promise

People who have seen the famous clip of a twisting bridge, or never heard of it, and half remember
"resonance" from school. In a minute they see why the Tacoma Narrows Bridge really fell, and they
learn that the textbook explanation was wrong.

## Angle

Candidates: a bridge that danced; the dog that was the only death; the wind did not have to gust;
textbooks got it wrong for decades; why bridges now go into wind tunnels. The strongest single line
is the textbook myth, but it needs the story first, so the dance is the hook and the myth is the twist.

## Story

The hero is the bridge itself, built in code. Line one: a new bridge that dances. It becomes a
local thrill (Galloping Gertie), then we see why it is so flexible (half a mile long, eight feet
deep). The wind arrives; the motion changes from bounce to twist; the mechanism gets twelve seconds:
each tilt lets the wind push it further. The real film proves it; a dog is trapped; the deck falls.
Twist: it wasn't resonance. Payoff: wind tunnels, so bridges "never learn to dance" — the first
line, answered. Beats: hook 9 s, ribbon 6, wind 6, twist 13, film 2, tubby 4, fall 3, myth 9, lesson 8.

## Hook

1. The whole bridge from the air, the deck already rippling: "In 1940, a brand-new bridge … started
to dance." 2. The real film of the collapse first. 3. A dog in a car on a twisting road. The first
wins: it is moving from frame one and holds the real film back for proof; the open question (why did
it dance?) is answered by the twist beat and the myth beat.

## World and hero

One world built in code with three.js: the Narrows on an overcast morning — water, two wooded shores,
the bridge with towers, cables, suspenders and a deck of 64 segments that bounce and twist from the
narration's clock; wind as streaks. The hero is the bridge. The abstract mechanism (flutter) is a
second code-built view: the deck end-on in the wind, the streaks bending over it and an arrow showing
the push. Real film enters twice, as evidence only: the twisting deck and the fall. No people built
from shapes; the car stands in for the man and the dog.

## Shot plan

- hook: aerial of the bridge, deck rippling; 1940; name tag; a chase camera riding the bouncing deck
  behind a car; GALLOPING GERTIE stamp. (code)
- ribbon: track along the span with 2,800 ft; side view of the thin deck with 8 ft; ripples. (code)
- wind: low over the water, wind streaks thicken; November 7, 1940; 40 mph. (code)
- twist: along the deck as it starts to twist; the section in the wind with the push arrow and an
  energy ring; the bridge writhing. (code)
- film: `collapse-1940` (find video "Tacoma Narrows" → Internet Archive, Tacoma Fire Department film,
  public domain), seconds 54–61, as a framed print.
- tubby: the abandoned car rolling with the deck; pointer TUBBY. (code)
- fall: the same film at the fall, the splash on "fell".
- myth: the section with gusting wind, RESONANCE crossed out; steady wind, the swing still feeding.
- lesson: a streamlined deck still in a wind tunnel; the bridge from the air, calm.
Music: `impending-boom` (find music "suspenseful building tension cinematic" → Kevin MacLeod, CC BY 4.0).

## Pacing

Written after the test build, from the person's review; measured with `lockstep pacing
tacoma-narrows`. 64.5 s in all: hook 9.3 s, ribbon 7.0, wind 6.6, twist 13.3, film 2.3, tubby 4.7,
fall 3.7, myth 8.9, lesson 8.5. The mechanism (twist) got its 13 s, but the real 1940 film of the
twisting deck was on screen 2.3 s with narration over all of it, and no beat held until the last
2.8 s. The review: the animation and the footage were right, the pacing was rushed where it should
not have been. As it should be, at about the same length (65 s): hook 7.5 s and ribbon 5 s (one
sentence fewer in each); film 6 s — the line, then 1.5 s held while the deck twists; fall 5 s with
the splash held 1 s; twist 13 s with 1 s held at the steepest tilt; the rest as built.

## Look

`overcast-1940`: the 3D world in the 1940 film's grey-blue light so the archive film drops in without
a jump; materials in real steel, concrete, water and forest colours; white captions, key words yellow.

## Sound and voice

af_heart at 1.15. "Impending Boom" by Kevin MacLeod: quiet for the first eight seconds, then tense
sixteenth notes under the rest, ducked under the voice. Whooshes on camera moves, a stamp on the
nickname, a riser into the energy, an impact and splash on the fall, a deny on "It wasn't".

## What would make this fail

A bridge model that looks like a toy — so it is shown in fog and soft light, with real proportions and
the real film as the reference. The mechanism turning into a lecture — so it is one picture (the
tilted deck and the push) held for twelve seconds. Repeating the myth — the script says plainly that
resonance is the wrong word and what is right.

## Retrospective

What worked, by the person's review of the test build: the animation built in code, the voice, the
real 1940 film used as evidence, and the music (Kevin MacLeod's "Impending Boom").

What did not: the pacing. The explanation and the real footage went by too fast for a viewer to
take in, while the setup had more time than it needed. The film of the twisting deck was on screen
2.3 s and talked over; the fall was talked over too. The fix is not a longer video but time moved
between beats (see Pacing above). This review is why the skill now plans pacing per beat in the
brief, prints it after recording (`lockstep pacing`), and lints real footage that is rushed.

Next time: plan the held moments in the brief before writing the script; give the real footage
4–8 s with the voice silent for part of it; check `lockstep pacing` before drawing any scene.
