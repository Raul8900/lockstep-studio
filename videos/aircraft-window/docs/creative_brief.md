# Creative brief — The tiny hole in aeroplane windows

Exact requested topic: **The tiny hole in aeroplane windows: Why that pin-sized bleed hole prevents the cabin window from shattering at 35,000 feet.**
The shattering phrase is the hook, not a universal promise or a failure threshold. The explanation concerns the documented Airbus arrangement. Production began only after PR #6 was confirmed open at c7f6946.

## Audience and promise
A curious passenger sees a pinhole and wonders whether the window leaks. In roughly forty-five seconds they will distinguish the protective scratch pane, the vented inner structural pane and the normally loaded outer pane. The familiar view becomes a comprehensible assembly.

## Raw material
Three physical layers; two structural panes; one passenger-facing protector; acrylic construction; a tiny vent; cabin-side pressure; an inter-pane gap; a normally loaded outer pane; an inner backup; a quiet cabin; a pressure differential; a return to the ordinary view. Most surprising: the hole loads the gap, the inner pane is a reserve, the surface you touch is not structural. Most drawable: three layers, airflow into the gap, load shifting between structural panes.

## Angle
Five possible angles: the hole is intentional; the window is three layers; pressure chooses a load path; the inner pane is a backup; the surface you touch is a protector. Choose pressure choosing a load path because it explains the hole and the redundancy together. Layer counting alone is a list, the protector alone misses the mechanism, and a dramatic failure story would misrepresent a normal flight.

## Directions considered
### Passenger zoom journey
Begin at the seat with a bright oval window and a visible dot behind the protective surface. Move closer, rotate the assembly into an exploded cutaway, follow air into the structural gap, then return to the seat. Quiet wonder suits an everyday observation. Its main risk is confusing a schematic separation with the actual pane spacing; label it as an enlarged schematic.
### An air molecule's journey
A friendly travelling dot starts beside a seat and hunts for a route into the window. A playful obstacle course reveals the vent before ending inside the gap. This is a character-first route with a soft illustrated palette. Its weakness is making the tiny continuous pressure communication seem like a single parcel of air, and encouraging an incorrect path through the scratch pane.
### A maintenance case file
Open with an already missing outer pane, ask which layer can still carry pressure, then rewind to normal operation and reveal the vent's role. A forensic notebook style makes the redundancy surprising. The counterfactual first structure is different, but its alarm and failure imagery could make a normal flight appear hazardous. It also delays the familiar passenger question.

## Chosen direction
Choose the passenger zoom journey, with one clearly labelled counterfactual borrowed from the maintenance case file near the end. The opening is an ordinary object rather than a diagram. Real Three.js geometry makes the same layers rotate, while native labels and pressure graphics explain them. The backup moment is short and explicitly conditional. It ends where it began, with understanding rather than alarm.

## Hook
Three candidates: a passenger window with “Why a hole?”; a split pane with “Which layer saves you?”; a giant dot with “This tiny hole manages pressure.” Choose the first: the picture is recognisable immediately and the line asks why a pressurised window contains a hole. The pressure explanation closes the question; the return to the seat supplies the emotional payoff. The cruise label says 35,000 ft, never a breaking threshold.

## Story shape
Four chapters follow the requested beat map. Hook, about five seconds: passenger view approaches the vent. Layers, about twelve seconds: rotate and separate the same three panes, identifying scratch, inner and outer in order. Pressure, about fourteen seconds: a cyan airflow path stops inside the gap, then an amber load graphic identifies the outer pane. Backup, about twelve seconds: a labelled hypothetical outer failure shifts load to the inner pane, before the ordinary view returns. The format change at the pressure beat separates motion of air from mechanical load.

## Pacing
Target 42–46 seconds within the requested 35–50. Voice af_heart at natural speed 1.0, compact sentences and short pauses between connected ideas. Hold each pane name for readability. Camera motion stays smooth. The last return gets a small breathing space, with no dramatic sting.

## Visual world
One original rounded aircraft window is the recurring object. A broad cabin surround, pale sky and soft cloud bands locate the opening in the passenger's world. The cutaway uses a real perspective camera, lit extruded acrylic surfaces, thickness and occlusion. Spacing and vent diameter are enlarged for clarity and labelled schematic. Leaders attach to physical coordinates on each pane. No airline logos, footage, copied drawings, flying glass or frightened passengers appear.

## Style
The candidate cabin-daylight uses warm white cabin trim, deep navy text, teal air, amber load, blue structural layers and muted red only for the conditional failure. Space Grotesk headlines and DM Sans labels feel like clear travel information. The alternate cabin-night uses a dark editorial stage with luminous surfaces. Preview both before choosing; daylight is the intended lead because this is routine engineering, not a crisis. Large captions sit below the assembly. Soft fades and a continuous orbit link the scenes.

## Sound
Choose the existing af_heart voice for a conversational question and a calm explanation. The score is intentionally silent, with only the original quiet filtered-air ambience at low gain. Lower the ambience further around the pressure explanation. Effects are silent: an explosive hiss would falsely suggest a leak outside. Narration remains the timing clock throughout.

## What would make this fail
Wrong layer assignment would invalidate the film: only the inner structural geometry contains a hole. An airflow arrow reaching outside would imply a leak: its endpoint stays between structural panes. A load arrow mistaken for airflow would obscure the mechanism: use a separate labelled amber graphic. Small labels or overlapping leaders would defeat phone viewing: inspect full-size frames and decoded contact sheets, and keep the model within the safe diagram band.

## Style comparison result
The rendered comparison favours cabin-daylight: its broad pale surfaces resemble cabin trim and make the labelled cutaway feel ordinary and readable. Cabin-night is legible but makes the question look like a failure investigation. Choose daylight.
