# Creative brief — “The Accident That Saved Millions”

## Audience and promise

People who know the word "penicillin" and maybe the phrase "discovered by accident", and nothing
else. In a minute they get the real story — the mess, the decade nobody cared, the dying
policeman, the melon, the war — and how the drug actually kills bacteria, ending on a warning
that is about them.

## Angle

Candidates: (1) the most important discovery was a dirty dish; (2) the first patient died because
they ran out; (3) a rotten melon from Illinois won the war's medicine race; (4) how penicillin
makes bacteria explode; (5) Fleming warned us about resistance in 1945. One story holds all five
in order, with (1) as the hook and (5) as the callback. Each alone is a fact; together they are a
plot with a turn every eight seconds.

## Story

Protagonist: Fleming at first, then the mould itself as it passes from hand to hand. Mystery in
the first line (which dish, why the world). Escalation: the bacteria are dead → why (the burst) →
ignored for ten years → purified, a dying man recovers → it runs out → recycled from his urine →
he dies → the hunt → the melon → millions of doses. Payoff: Fleming's warning came true, and the
last line calls back to the dirty dishes. Beats: hook 5 s, plate 4 s, burst 9 s, shelf 6 s,
oxford 8 s, urine 6 s, melon 7 s, dday 3 s, warning 9 s — about 62 s at 190 words.

## Hook

1. Fleming at his bench, the camera starting on the clutter of dishes: "In 1928, a scientist came
back from holiday to a pile of dirty dishes." 2. A petri dish filling the frame: "This mould
has saved more lives than any medicine in history." 3. A dying policeman: "In 1941, the first
patient ever treated with penicillin… died." The first wins: a real photograph of the real man in
the real lab, and a line with a double meaning (dishes = culture plates) that the end pays off.

## World and hero

The archive: photographs and film of the 1920s–40s — the lab, the men, the infirmary, the
wartime production, D-Day — graded warm and grainy as one film. The hero is the mould itself; Fleming
is its first keeper. The one abstract idea, how penicillin kills, becomes a physical thing in 3D:
the bacterium is a capsule wrapped in a wall of bricks that keep being added as it grows;
glowing penicillin molecules jam the bricklaying, bricks fall away, and the cell bursts. Left
out: Chain's chemistry, Heatley's apparatus, the Nobel, the strain names.

## Shot plan

- hook: Fleming at work in his lab (find photo "Alexander Fleming laboratory", Wikimedia, public
  domain) — starts tight on the dishes, pulls back to him; name tag on "scientist".
- plate: a Penicillium culture photo (find photo "Penicillium notatum culture") pushing in;
  a hand-drawn circle round the clear ring on "dead"; a bacteria micrograph if it reads.
- burst: 3D world built in code (no media): the brick-walled bacterium, penicillin arriving,
  the wall cracking, the burst on "bursts" with a flash and an impact.
- shelf: Fleming's 1929 paper (find photo "Fleming penicillin 1929 paper") with Fleming cut out
  of a portrait in front of it; stamp "IGNORED" on "nobody".
- oxford: Radcliffe Infirmary or the Oxford team (find photo "Radcliffe Infirmary Oxford",
  "Howard Florey"); a ring gauge for the supply, draining to zero on "ran out".
- urine: the same infirmary picture, darker; spotlight; fade to near black on "He died".
- melon: a cantaloupe (find photo "cantaloupe") with a pointer "PEORIA, ILLINOIS"; wartime
  production photographs (find photo "penicillin production 1943").
- dday: the D-Day landing photograph (find photo "Omaha Beach landing June 1944", US Coast Guard,
  public domain) with a count-up of "millions"; the 1944 penicillin poster if found.
- warning: Fleming's portrait pushing in; a bacteria micrograph on "resist"; the final shot
  returns to the lab photo, pushing in on the dishes — the callback.
Where two portraits or two D-Day photographs are equally good, the person is asked to choose.

## Pacing

Written after the build, when per-beat pacing became part of the brief; the numbers are measured
(`lockstep pacing penicillin-accident`). 68 s in all. Setup beats are brisk: plate 5 s, dday 2.7 s.
The mechanism (burst) gets 10 s at a slower 170 words a minute and the Oxford rescue 10 s; the
warning beat ends on 2.5 s held on the closing photograph. What it lacks, by today's rule: no held
moment inside the story — the burst is talked over from start to end, and the D-Day photograph is
on screen under 3 s. A rebuild would hold 1.5 s at the burst's peak and give D-Day 4 s, paid for by
trimming the hook (7.6 s) and the melon beat (7.7 s), keeping the total.

## Look

`penicillin-archive`: archive photographs warmed and slightly desaturated with a little sepia, a
deep vignette, so 1920s plates, 1940s posters and a 3D render read as one old film; white
captions with the key word in yellow; cuts, with a flash on the reveals.

## Sound and voice

af_heart at 1.15 (about 190 words a minute): warm but quick. Music from a held drone through a
tense middle, building on Oxford, dark at the death, driving through the melon, bright at D-Day,
resolving at the warning. Whooshes on camera moves, an impact on the burst, a stamp on IGNORED,
a riser into the warning.

## What would make this fail

Archive photos held still would feel like a slideshow — every shot moves and cuts on its word.
The mechanism could turn into a lecture — it is nine seconds of one physical event. The death
could feel exploitative — it is told plainly, on a photograph of the hospital, not of a body.

## Retrospective

What worked: the archive carried the story — a colour photograph of Fleming at his bench opens and
closes it, and every beat has a real picture of the real thing (the 1929 paper, Florey, the 1943
Oxford production, the D-Day landing, resistant bacteria). The camera never stops, and the one
abstract idea (how penicillin kills) became one physical event in 3D. The callback to the dirty
dishes lands on the same photograph as the hook.

What did not: there is no free photograph of Fleming's own plate, so the plate shot is a Penicillium
culture with the bacteria drawn on; the patient beat shows 1943 production rather than the 1941
ward; Wikimedia rate-limited original downloads, so a few pictures are its 1920 px copies.
Several pictures were close calls the person would normally choose (rule 3): the plate, the
cantaloupe, which D-Day photograph.

Next time: ask for the close calls before scenes are written; look for public-domain film (the
Internet Archive has 1940s newsreels on penicillin) to open on moving pictures; budget a minute
for the 3D shot's lighting, which took two passes.
