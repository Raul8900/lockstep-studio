# Sources — “The Accident That Saved Millions”

| Claim (spoken or on screen) | Source |
|---|---|
| In 1928 Fleming came back from holiday to a stack of culture plates; on one, a mould had grown and the staphylococci around it had been killed | Britannica, “How did Alexander Fleming discover penicillin?” — https://www.britannica.com/question/How-did-Alexander-Fleming-discover-penicillin ; Wikipedia, “Discovery of penicillin” — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discovery_of_penicillin (dates differ between accounts: 3 or 28 September 1928; the video says only 1928) |
| He named the substance penicillin and published in 1929 (British Journal of Experimental Pathology) | the paper itself, first page shown on screen (Wellcome Collection, CC BY 4.0: media/credits.json) |
| How penicillin kills: bacteria keep rebuilding their cell wall as they grow; penicillin blocks the enzymes that build it, the wall weakens and the cell bursts | standard mechanism (inhibition of the transpeptidases that cross-link peptidoglycan; lysis of growing cells) — e.g. Wikipedia, “Penicillin” — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penicillin#Mechanism_of_action. The “builders” and “bricks” are a metaphor for the enzymes and the wall's cross-linked chains |
| He could make little of it, and for about ten years little came of it | Wikipedia, “History of penicillin” — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_penicillin ; Oxford's Florey–Chain work began in 1939 |
| In Oxford, Florey's team purified it; their first patient treated for an infection was Albert Alexander, a police constable (12 February 1941), who improved within days; the supply ran out; they recovered penicillin from his urine to reuse it; he relapsed and died in March 1941 | Wikipedia, “Albert Alexander (police officer)” — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Alexander_(police_officer) ; University of Oxford, “75 years of penicillin in people” — https://www.ox.ac.uk/node/213547 ; Oxfordshire Blue Plaques — https://www.oxonblueplaques.org.uk/plaques/penicillin_infirmary.html |
| The best production strain came from a mouldy cantaloupe from a Peoria, Illinois fruit market (USDA laboratory) | The Scientist, “How a Moldy Cantaloupe Brought Fleming's Penicillin to the World” — https://www.the-scientist.com/how-a-moldy-cantaloupe-took-fleming-s-penicillin-from-discovery-to-mass-production-72304 ; USDA ARS, “The Enduring Mystery of ‘Moldy Mary’” — https://scientificdiscoveries.ars.usda.gov/tellus/stories/articles/enduring-mystery-moldy-mary (who found it is disputed; the video does not name anyone) |
| By D-Day there were millions of doses | University of Wisconsin–Madison, “D-Day invasion was bolstered by UW–Madison penicillin project” — https://news.wisc.edu/?p=38190 (“millions of doses”; no exact figure is used, since sources disagree) |
| Fleming warned that misuse — too-low doses — would make bacteria resistant | his Nobel lecture, 1945, as quoted by the Nobel Prize's own Q&A — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1945/fleming/questions-and-answers/ |
| The bacteria in the last scene are resistant (MRSA) | the micrograph's own description: NIAID, “Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus Aureus (MRSA) Bacteria”, CC BY 2.0 |

Media and their licences: `media/credits.json`; the credit lines for the description: `docs/credits.md`.

Checked and not used: the story that Albert Alexander's infection began with a rose-thorn scratch
(widely repeated, doubted by some historians); “2.3 million doses by D-Day” (not found in a source
we could check); “200 million lives saved” (an estimate without a clear source).
