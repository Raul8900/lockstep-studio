## Audience and promise

Anyone who has looked at a full Moon and assumed it is fixed in place. In fifteen seconds they learn that
it is leaving, how fast, why, and how anyone could possibly know — and they can repeat the mirror fact to a friend.

## Angle

Candidates: the Moon is leaving us; a day used to last 22 hours; there are mirrors on the Moon; the tides are a
brake on Earth; one day there will be no total eclipses. The mirrors are the most surprising but need a setup; the
Moon leaving is the strongest first line and the mirrors become its proof. The others are true but slower to show.

## Story

The protagonist is the Moon, and the viewer is the one being left. Line one states the mystery (it is running away),
line two gives the scale and the cause in one breath, line three asks the viewer's own question (“how do we know?”)
and answers it with a real object astronauts left behind, ending on the present tense: we still fire lasers at it.
Beats: hook (2 s, the claim), drift (5 s, the number and the tides), proof (6 s, the mirrors and the laser).

## Hook

1. The full Moon over a city, the camera pulling away from it: “The Moon is slowly running away from us.”
2. A laser hitting the Moon: “We are shooting lasers at the Moon.” 3. A calendar: “Days used to be shorter.”
The first wins: the picture moves the way the sentence says, and the question it opens (why, and how do we know)
is answered by the end.

## World and hero

One world: NASA's own record of the Moon — its photographs, its film, and its maps wrapped on a 3D Earth and Moon.
The Moon is the hero in every shot. The abstract part (tides pushing an orbit) becomes physical as a pale ocean
shell bulging towards the Moon. Left out: angular momentum, Earth's slowing day, the numbers behind the timing.

## Shot plan

- hook: photo `moon-capitol` (find photo "full moon" → NASA, public domain); camera pulls back from the Moon to the city.
- drift: 3D world, Earth and Moon textured with `earth-map` (find photo "blue marble equirectangular" → NASA, public domain)
  and `moon-map` (Solar System Scope, CC BY 4.0); camera high behind the Earth, the Moon sliding away above it;
  bigNumber 3.8 cm on “four”; the tide shell and a pointer on “tides”.
- proof: film `apollo-moonwalk` (find video "Apollo 11 moonwalk" → NASA, public domain, 4 s cut), then photo
  `retroreflector` (find photo "Apollo 11 laser ranging retroreflector" → NASA, public domain) pushing in on the mirror,
  a pointer on “mirrors”, a green beam on “lasers”. Gaps: none.

## Pacing

About 17 seconds. The hook is brisk (the claim, about 2.5 s) and the drift beat is one sentence over
the 3D Earth and Moon (about 5 s). The proof is the payoff and the only real footage, so it gets
the most time: the Apollo film plays through its line and then for a held 1.2 s with the voice
silent, about 4.5 s on screen, before the cut to the mirror; the laser lands with 1.4 s of the
photograph held at the end. No beat is slowed down: the explanation is a single sentence here.

## Look

`night-archive`: NASA's pictures graded a little cooler and contrastier with a faint blue tint so the orange Moon, the
black-and-white film and the 3D render read as one film; default white captions with the key word in yellow.

## Sound and voice

af_heart at 1.15 (about 190 words a minute): calm and certain. Music from drone to build to finale; a whoosh as the
camera pulls away, an impact on the number, a zip and a chime when the laser lands.

## What would make this fail

The number alone is forgettable — so it lands on the word and the Moon visibly moves. Tides are easy to get wrong —
so the line only says they push it, and the picture shows the bulge leaning ahead. Fifteen seconds of space can look
like a screensaver — so the camera never stops and the last shot is a real object with a real beam on it.
