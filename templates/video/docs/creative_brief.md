# Creative brief — “__TITLE__”

This is the brief for the starter video that ships with the template: an 18-second piece about the
one rule this engine is built on. It doubles as a worked example of the depth each section needs.

## Audience and promise

People who make or study short explainers and have felt that most of them blur together. They
already believe that "good editing" or "good visuals" is what holds attention. After 18 seconds
they should be able to name a concrete, checkable rule — every picture lands on the word that
names it — and notice when a video breaks it. They stop scrolling because the opening question is
about the very thing they are doing at that moment: scrolling.

## Angle

Candidates: (1) attention is earned in the first second; (2) captions matter more than footage;
(3) one idea per sentence; (4) picture and voice must move in lockstep; (5) sound design is
half the picture. Kept (4): it is true, it is the least obvious of the five, and it can be shown
literally — the video can draw its own narration track and drop a picture onto a word. (1) and
(3) are folded in as the first beat; (2) and (5) are too abstract to draw in 18 seconds.

## Directions considered

### A — The frozen feed (chosen)

A phone feed of grey skeleton posts flicks past at speed and stops dead on one card exactly as the
voice says "impossible". The card lights up, the camera dives into it, and inside is the rule
demonstrated on the video's own narration track. It ends with voice and picture locked on one
playhead. Suits the topic because the opening is the viewer's own gesture. Risk: a skeleton feed can
look like a generic app mock-up unless the stop is sharp and the colour change is decisive.

### B — The metronome

A single metronome ticks under the narration; every word is a tick, every picture snaps in on a
tick. Musical, minimal, very clear about timing. Risk: it explains rhythm rather than meaning, and a
metronome reads as "music lesson", which pulls the viewer's attention away from explainers.

### C — Before / after split screen

The same sentence played twice, top half with the picture arriving late, bottom half arriving on the
word, so the viewer feels the difference. Very persuasive in a longer video. Risk: two simultaneous
pictures at phone size halve the readable area, and 18 seconds is too short to play anything twice.

## Chosen direction

A. It is the only one whose first frame is already the argument: the viewer is mid-scroll and the
video scrolls with them, then refuses to be scrolled past. B's tick-per-word grid is borrowed for the
narration track in the second beat, so the timing idea is still drawn literally. C is dropped: no room
to play anything twice at this length.

## Hook

Three openings were written. (1) Feed flicks past and freezes on "impossible", line: "What makes an
explainer impossible to scroll past?" (2) A thumb icon swiping upward with a "SKIP" counter, line: "You
skip ninety percent of videos in a second." (3) Black frame with one ticking word, line: "Timing."
Chose (1): the picture and the question are the same gesture, it needs no statistic we would have to
source, and the freeze lands on the strongest word. The open loop is "what makes it impossible?",
closed at "in lockstep".

## Story shape

A three-beat question → demonstration → rule. Hook (0–3.5 s): the feed scrolls and freezes, question
asked, "ONE RULE ↓" promises an answer. Idea (3.5–12 s): pattern interrupt by diving into the card;
first one dot out of five becomes the idea, then a narration track where the picture lands on the word
"word" while early and late ghosts are crossed out on "before" and "after". Payoff (12–18 s): voice and
picture panels wake one after the other and snap together under one playhead; the end card holds
the rule. The ending mirrors the start: motion that refused to stop, then motion that is locked.

## Pacing

About 18 seconds, 34 words, af_heart at speed 1.0. Pauses short inside beats (0.25–0.45 s) and a
0.6–0.7 s breath at each scene change. Something visibly changes at least every 1.5 seconds, mostly
on stressed words. The narration track needs about two seconds of steady motion to be read, so
"Every picture lands on the exact word" is delivered in one even take.

## Visual world

An editor's desk: graph paper, cards, a timeline — the tools people use to make videos, which is
what the video is about. The hero object is the feed card: it is what the viewer scrolls, what the
camera dives into, and the shape of every panel afterwards. Diagrams are flat and literal (a real
narration track built from the voice's own word timings); type carries the rule. Two
illustrations act: the viewer's thumb flicking the feed (it freezes with it, then startles), and a
speaking head and a clapperboard that hop together when voice and picture lock. No phone frame,
logos or real app interfaces — a skeleton feed is enough to read as "a feed".

## Style

studio-grid: a light theme, chosen against the dark neon of the reference video so the starter
shows the opposite end of the system. Warm off-white graph paper (the desk), electric blue as
subject (ink of a working document), orange for the one idea in focus, violet for the voice, red for
wrong timing, green for "locked". Space Grotesk for headlines (precise, slightly quirky), DM Sans for
body, Space Mono for labels (timecode feel). Grid backdrop drifting slowly; white cards with soft
shadows; illustrations from the flat set with the same soft shadow, so they sit on the desk like the
cards; box-highlighted captions; a thin progress line instead of chapter labels (three chapters
do not need names); slide transitions by default. Rejected: chalkboard (reads as a lesson, wrong
for makers) and poster-pop (too loud for a calm rule).

## Sound

100 BPM, default synth kit, moods drone → light → finale so the music goes from suspended to
resolved as the rule is stated. Effects: ticks while the feed scrolls, a lock and impact on the
freeze, a tick on every spoken word in the narration track, a low buzz on each crossed-out ghost,
a lock and chime when voice and picture merge. Cues on short words are placed slightly early and
kept quiet so they never mask the word.

## What would make this fail

It looks like a generic UI mock-up: prevented by the decisive freeze and the dive into the card.
The rule sounds like a platitude: prevented by drawing it on the video's own word timings, so the
claim is literally true on screen. Light theme washes out on a phone: style-check contrast passes
for every role, and captions keep an outline plus a highlight box.
